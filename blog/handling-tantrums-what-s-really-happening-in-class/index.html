Handling Tantrums: What’s Really Happening in Class

Practical strategies for teachers in Bengaluru schools to manage classroom meltdowns with calm, connection, and simple routine tweaks.

Nidhi, Samhithe · Nov 24, 2026 · 5 min read

The Meltdown in the Middle of Circle Time

It is circle time. The children are settling in, mats are arranged, and the teacher is about to start the morning story. Suddenly, a child drops to the floor, screaming, kicking, or shutting down completely. The room goes silent. Other children look confused or frightened. The teacher feels a spike of adrenaline, embarrassment, and perhaps a hint of personal failure. In that split second, the entire atmosphere of the classroom shifts from warm and inviting to tense and chaotic.

For many educators, this scenario is not an occasional hiccup but a recurring reality. Whether it is during snack time, transitions between activities, or when a simple instruction is given, emotional dysregulation can derail even the best-laid lesson plans. The immediate reaction is often to stop the behavior as quickly as possible to restore order. However, viewing these moments purely as disruptions misses the underlying communication. When a child “melts down,” they are not trying to be difficult; they are struggling to cope with a demand that exceeds their current capacity to handle it.

Decoding the Behavior: It Is Not Just “Bad Moods”

To handle a tantrum effectively, we must first shift our perspective from punishment to understanding. A tantrum is a symptom, not the root cause. Children, especially in early years and lower primary grades, lack the neurological wiring to verbally articulate complex feelings like frustration, overwhelm, or fear. When their internal pressure valve gets too high, the only outlet left is physical or vocal expression.

Every behavior serves a purpose. When a child acts out, they are communicating an unmet need or an inability to manage a specific trigger. This could be sensory overload from a noisy assembly, anxiety about an upcoming change in routine, or a genuine difficulty with a task that feels too hard. By recognizing that the behavior is a form of communication, we move away from asking “How do I make this stop?” and toward asking “What is this child trying to tell me?”

The Power of Connection Before Correction

The most critical moment in handling a tantrum is the first thirty seconds. When a child is in the throes of an emotional storm, their thinking brain—the part responsible for logic, reasoning, and learning—has essentially gone offline. The emotional brain is in charge. Attempting to reason with, lecture, or punish a child in this state is like trying to explain calculus to someone who is running for their life. It will not work.

Instead, the priority must be co-regulation. This means the teacher acts as an external regulator for the child’s nervous system. This does not mean giving in to demands or ignoring safety boundaries. It means staying calm, lowering your voice, and offering a presence that signals safety. A simple, calm statement like, “I see you are upset. I am here with you,” can begin to bridge the gap. This approach builds trust. It tells the child that their feelings are valid, even if their behavior needs boundaries. Connection before correction ensures that the child feels safe enough to eventually re-engage with learning.

Immediate Strategies for the Moment

When the meltdown is happening, practical steps can help de-escalate the situation without adding to the chaos:

  • Stay Low and Calm: Kneel down to the child’s eye level. This reduces the perceived threat and makes you less intimidating. Speak in a low, slow, and rhythmic tone.
  • Reduce Sensory Input: If possible, move the child away from the center of attention. A quiet corner, a hallway, or a designated “calming space” can provide the necessary reduction in stimuli.
  • Offer Minimal Choices: Instead of open-ended questions, offer two simple, safe choices. “Would you like to sit here or there?” This restores a sense of control without overwhelming the child.
  • Use Visual Supports: For some children, a visual cue or a simple object can be more effective than words. A “first–then” board or a visual schedule can help clarify what is happening next.

Proactive Changes to Routine and Environment

While managing the immediate incident is crucial, the real work happens in prevention. Many tantrums are predictable if we look at the context. Transitions are often the most vulnerable times in a school day. Moving from play to lunch, or from outdoor play to assembly, requires significant cognitive flexibility. Children with sensory differences or anxiety may find these shifts jarring.

Small tweaks to routines can make a massive difference. Using visual schedules that show the sequence of the day helps children anticipate what comes next. Incorporating movement breaks can help release pent-up energy before it builds into frustration. Even simple changes, like giving instructions one at a time rather than in a long list, can reduce cognitive overload. These adjustments are not special treatments; they are universal design principles that support all children in maintaining their emotional equilibrium.

Identifying Early Signs and Supporting Development

Teachers are on the front lines of observation. You are the ones who see the child day in and day out. You might notice that a certain child consistently struggles with group settings, or that they become aggressive when their workspace is cluttered. These are not just “bad habits”; they are data points. Recognizing early signs of sensory differences, anxiety, or social-emotional delays allows for timely support.

It is important to approach these observations without labeling. A child is not “defiant” or “disruptive.” They are a learner who may need different tools to navigate their environment. By documenting these patterns—what happens before, during, and after the behavior—teachers can create a clearer picture of the child’s needs. This information is invaluable for creating an inclusive classroom where every child feels understood and supported.

Building a Supportive School Culture

Handling tantrums is not just an individual teacher’s responsibility; it is a school-wide culture. When all staff members—from the principal to the playground supervisor—understand the same framework for behavior, children experience consistency. This consistency is deeply reassuring for children who struggle with emotional regulation.

Schools that prioritize social-emotional learning create environments where mistakes are seen as learning opportunities. When teachers feel supported and equipped with the right tools, they are less likely to experience burnout. They can respond to challenging behaviors with empathy and skill rather than frustration. This shift benefits everyone: children feel safer, teachers feel more competent, and the overall learning environment becomes more conducive to growth.

Conclusion

Tantrums are a natural part of childhood development, but they do not have to be disruptive to the learning environment. By shifting our focus from controlling behavior to understanding needs, we can transform these moments into opportunities for connection and growth. Simple, proactive strategies and a compassionate, consistent approach can help children develop the emotional skills they need to thrive.

Frequently asked questions

How should a teacher respond when a child is screaming during class?

Stay calm and low, reduce sensory input by moving the child to a quieter space, and offer a safe presence without trying to reason until they are regulated.

What are some proactive ways to prevent tantrums during transitions?

Use visual schedules, give warnings before changes, and incorporate movement breaks to help children prepare mentally and physically for the next activity.

How can teachers tell if a tantrum is due to sensory issues?

Look for patterns where the child reacts strongly to specific sounds, lights, or textures, or becomes overwhelmed in crowded or noisy environments.
